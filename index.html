Chapter Two — Interior Design

Residential interiors, room by room.

Every room answers a different question — how a family gathers, how someone sleeps, how a kitchen actually gets used. Organised that way here: not project by project, but room by room, so what stays consistent across projects is easier to see. Click any image to view it larger.

Living Room

This is the room that has to work the hardest — everyday evenings and the rare dinner party, both. The instinct across these is the same: one warm, tactile anchor — a carved wood panel, a floral accent wall, a mandala carving — that gives the eye somewhere to land, kept honest by a neutral floor and ceiling so the room doesn't compete with itself. Seating is arranged to face each other, not just the television, because a living room that only works for watching TV isn't really a living room.

Dining Room

Dining spaces get treated as part of the living room, not a separate errand — same material language, same sightlines, so a family can eat together without leaving the conversation. A round or oval table softens what's usually the most rectilinear room in the house, and pendant lighting is pulled low enough to make even a Tuesday dinner feel a little considered.

The Sanctuary — Where the Day Actually Ends

A living room performs; a bedroom recovers — and that one difference decides almost everything about how these are designed. Colour does real work here, not decoration: a cooler blue or sage slows the eye and, by extension, the mind, which is why it turns up in rooms built for children and study, where calm needs to be built into the walls rather than negotiated every night. A deeper maroon or rust does the opposite — it wraps a room in a kind of confident warmth, better suited to a primary bedroom where the person living there wants to feel held, not just soothed. Quieter, more neutral palettes are reserved for guest rooms on purpose — comfortable enough to relax in, restrained enough that a guest never mistakes hospitality for someone else's identity. Across all of them the same rule holds: less visual noise, more considered light, and a wardrobe wall detailed enough to disappear once the lights go down. A bedroom's job is never to impress. It's to let a person stop being on.

Kitchen

Kitchens here are planned around the two people actually using them, not the photograph — enough counter run for prep and plating without a single unnecessary step between the stove, the sink and the fridge. Sage greens, warm woods and dark countertops repeat because they hide daily use gracefully; a kitchen that looks perfect only on day one has already failed its brief.

Foyer & Entry

The five seconds between the front door and the rest of the home set the tone for everything after — so entries get one deliberate gesture: a carved wood door, a gold-framed mirror, a floral accent wall — something to notice on the way in, before the practical business of shoe storage and coat hooks takes over just behind it.

Wardrobe & Storage

The part nobody sees in the render but everyone feels every morning. Every shutter, drawer and shelf is planned around what actually goes in it — hanging space sized to real garment length, drawers where hands reach first, a loft for the things used twice a year. Drawn, dimensioned and specified before it's ever cut, so what a carpenter builds matches exactly what a client approved.

Kitchen Elevations

A kitchen lives or dies on the numbers most people never think about — counter height, shutter clearance, where the chimney sits relative to the hob. These sheets carry every one of them, laminate codes included, so there's no improvising on-site and no surprise on installation day.

Mandir & Pooja Unit

A small unit that carries a disproportionate amount of meaning, so it gets a disproportionate amount of care — jali screens cut to a precise pattern, lighting planned to be soft rather than bright, proportions worked out in elevation long before the 3D view confirms it feels right.

TV Unit & Living Joinery

The single wall a living room is built around, so it's drawn with the same rigor as any structural element — panel widths, backlighting channels, shelf depths — because a feature wall that looks effortless in the render only stays effortless if every dimension behind it was actually planned.

Study & Home Office

Desk height, cable routing, shelf depth for a monitor or a stack of books — the unglamorous specifics that decide whether a home office actually gets used or quietly becomes a dumping ground. Worked out here before a single board is cut.

Foyer & Entry Storage

The first joinery a visitor ever sees, so it has to do two jobs at once — hold real shoe count and hide the household's inverter and battery discreetly, while still reading as a considered piece of furniture rather than a utility box. Finished to match whatever's visible from the door.

Electrical & Ceiling Layout

The drawing that never makes it into a portfolio but decides how a room actually feels — every panel light, spotlight, chandelier point and ceiling fan positioned and dimensioned before the false ceiling closes over it, coordinated room by room so an electrician has zero decisions left to improvise on site.
